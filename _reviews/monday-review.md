## The problem: the plan lives in five different places, and none of them agree

Most teams don't decide to adopt project management software so much as get pushed into it. A project starts in a shared spreadsheet because that's what everyone already has open. Status updates move to a Slack thread because the spreadsheet doesn't notify anyone of changes. Deadlines get tracked in someone's calendar, half-remembered from a meeting three weeks ago. Then a deliverable slips, and the postmortem question is always some version of "wait, who was supposed to own that?" — a question that a shared spreadsheet, by design, has no good way of answering.

The five tools below all solve that same underlying problem — one system of record for who owns what and when it's due — but they solve it with genuinely different underlying data models, and that difference matters more than any single feature comparison. A tool built around flexible databases and free-form docs behaves very differently day to day than one built around a fixed kanban board, even when both technically have a "board view." Picking based on brand recognition or a demo video, without checking whether the tool's actual structure matches how your team plans work, is the most common reason a company ends up paying for project management software that half the team quietly avoids opening.

That mismatch is also why switching tools rarely fixes the underlying problem on its own — a team that doesn't consistently update status in a spreadsheet usually won't consistently update status in a $19-a-seat tool either, unless the tool's structure makes updating status the path of least resistance rather than an extra chore. The comparison below tries to separate "what this tool is actually built to do" from "what it costs," since conflating the two is where most of the buyer's-remorse stories in this category come from.

## Three things that actually matter when you're choosing

**1. The underlying data model, not just the view options.** Monday.com and Trello are built around a board as the core unit — rows and cards that move through columns or lists, with Monday.com adding heavier customization (formulas, dependencies, automations) on top of that same board metaphor. ClickUp is built as an all-in-one workspace where tasks, docs, whiteboards, and goals are all first-class objects sitting inside the same hierarchy. Notion flips the model entirely — it's a flexible database-and-docs tool first, where "project management" is one of many things you can configure a workspace to do, rather than the workspace's built-in purpose. Asana sits closer to Monday.com and Trello (task-and-project-first) but with a more opinionated structure around goals, workflows, and cross-team reporting. None of these models is inherently better — a team that thinks in freeform docs will fight Trello's rigid cards, and a team that wants a simple kanban board will find Notion's database setup like assembling furniture before using it.

**2. What happens to the price as the team actually grows.** This is where the five diverge sharply. Monday.com and Asana Advanced require every paid seat to be on the same plan (no mixing full members and cheaper limited seats), and Monday.com's paid tiers carry a 3-seat minimum, so a 2-person team pays for a phantom third seat. ClickUp, Notion, and Trello have no published seat minimums, which matters for a small team not ready to commit to a fixed headcount. Guest and viewer access is the other lever worth checking: Monday.com's Basic plan includes unlimited viewers at no extra cost, while some competitors charge for any account with access, even read-only ones.

**3. What the free tier actually lets a small team do before anyone has to pay.** This varies more than the marketing pages suggest — some free tiers are a real, permanently usable product for a small team, and others are closer to a sample.

### What free actually gets you

| Tool | Free tier | What it actually gives you |
|---|---|---|
| Monday.com | Yes, up to 2 seats | 3 boards, docs, and 200+ templates — usable for a very small team, but the 2-seat cap and 3-board limit make it a short-term option rather than a long-term free plan |
| ClickUp | Yes, permanent | Unlimited tasks across 5 Spaces, kanban and calendar views, no seat cap — but only 60MB of total storage, which fills up fast with attachments |
| Notion | Yes, permanent | A full personal or small-team workspace with unlimited pages and databases — collaboration features (custom forms, unlimited file uploads) require Plus once the team grows |
| Asana | Yes, up to 2 users | Unlimited tasks, projects, and list/board/calendar views for a 2-person team — but no timeline view, dependencies, or automation |
| Trello | Yes, up to 10 collaborators per Workspace | Unlimited cards and up to 10 boards per Workspace — genuinely enough for a small team's entire kanban setup, not just a sample |

If a genuinely permanent, no-catch free tier for a small team matters most, ClickUp and Trello are the two of the five where "free" isn't capped at two seats.

### ClickUp

**What it's for:** teams trying to replace several separate tools (task tracker, docs, whiteboard, goal tracking) with one subscription, and comfortable with a steeper initial learning curve in exchange for that consolidation.

### Notion

**What it's for:** teams that already live in docs and wikis and want lightweight project tracking layered on top of that, rather than a dedicated PM tool with a rigid task structure.

<div class="pros-cons" markdown="1">
<div class="pros" markdown="1">

**Pros**

- The same workspace handles docs, wikis, and databases alongside task tracking, so a team already using Notion for documentation doesn't need a second tool just for task status

**Cons**

### Trello

**What it's for:** small teams or individuals who want the simplest possible kanban board — cards moving across lists — without needing dependencies, automation rules, or heavy configuration.

## Which one fits your situation

**Want heavy automation and a highly visual board, and don't mind paying for it:** Monday.com's Standard or Pro tier, budgeting for the 3-seat minimum and treating AI credits as a separate line item rather than assuming they're bundled into the seat price.

**Want to consolidate several tools (tasks, docs, whiteboards) into one subscription:** ClickUp's Unlimited or Business plan — the lowest entry price in this group for teams willing to spend real setup time learning the full feature set.

**Already live in docs and wikis, and want lightweight task tracking layered on top:** Notion Plus, especially if the team is already paying for Notion as a documentation tool and doesn't want a second, separate PM subscription.

**Need structured workflows and strong cross-team reporting, especially for marketing or ops:** Asana, using the 30-day trial on Starter or Advanced to test the real workflow before committing to a plan.

**Want the simplest possible board for a small team, at the lowest cost:** Trello — the free tier alone genuinely covers a small team's kanban needs, and Standard is the cheapest paid step up in this comparison if the team outgrows the free limits.

**Not sure which structure fits, and want to test before spending anything:** Start with Trello's free tier for a straightforward board and Notion's free tier for a docs-first alternative in the same week. Between the two you'll get a real feel for rigid-board versus flexible-database planning, which is the biggest factor in which of these five tools a team actually keeps using.

## Common questions

**Do guest or viewer accounts count toward the per-seat price?**
It depends on the tool. Monday.com includes unlimited viewer seats on every paid plan, so read-only stakeholders don't add to the bill. Other tools in this comparison vary by plan on how guest access is priced, so it's worth confirming directly on the pricing page before assuming viewer-only access is free.

**Is the free tier enough for a small team long-term, or just a trial?**
Trello and ClickUp's free tiers are built to be used indefinitely by a small team, with no time limit — Trello caps at 10 collaborators and 10 boards per Workspace, ClickUp caps at 60MB of storage. Monday.com and Asana's free tiers cap at 2 seats, which makes them workable for a very small team but not a long-term option once a third person needs access.

**How much does per-seat pricing actually change as a team grows?**
Every tool here gets somewhat cheaper per seat on annual billing versus monthly, typically by 20-30%. Monday.com's 3-seat minimum on paid plans is the one structural detail that changes the math specifically for very small teams, since it applies regardless of how many people actually need access.

**Can I migrate from a spreadsheet into any of these easily?**
All five support CSV import for basic task and project data, though how well titles, due dates, and assignees map over automatically varies by tool and is worth testing with a real (not sample) spreadsheet before committing a whole team's data to the migration.

**Which of these is easiest to onboard a completely non-technical team?**
Trello's card-and-list model has the shortest learning curve of the five. Monday.com and ClickUp both trade a longer setup and onboarding period for more powerful automation once a team is up to speed, which is a reasonable tradeoff for a team planning to stick with one tool long-term but a real cost if speed of adoption matters more right now.

## Where to start

Given how differently these five actually structure a project underneath the board view, the lowest-risk starting point is testing with a real, current project rather than a demo template — spreadsheets and sample data hide exactly the friction points (guest access, automation limits, storage caps) that show up once a team is actually using the tool daily.

Monday.com's free plan and 14-day Pro trial only need a work email to start free, which is enough to run one real board through a normal week before deciding whether the automation and customization are worth the per-seat price. If the board-first model doesn't feel right after that, Trello's free tier and Notion's free tier are both worth testing in the same sitting — between the three you'll get a genuine feel for board-first, docs-first, and all-in-one planning before spending anything.
