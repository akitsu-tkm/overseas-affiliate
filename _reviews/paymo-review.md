## The problem: three tools bolted together, none of them talking to each other

Freelancers and small agencies tend to arrive at this category the same way: a spreadsheet for tracking billable hours, a separate app for keeping tasks organized, and a third tool — or an accountant's inbox — for turning all of that into an invoice a client will actually pay. Each piece works fine in isolation. The friction shows up at the seams: hours logged in one tool that don't automatically become a line item in another, a project status that's accurate in the task board but stale in whatever the client sees, an invoice that has to be manually rebuilt from a timesheet export at the end of the month.

The five tools below all try to close that gap, but they close it from different starting points. Some genuinely combine project management, time tracking, and invoicing into one system where a tracked hour flows straight into a client invoice without a re-entry step. Others are time trackers first, with project structure or billing bolted on as a secondary feature — which is a perfectly reasonable choice if a team already has a project management tool it likes and just needs accurate hours feeding into it. Picking the wrong starting point is how a solo consultant ends up paying for a five-seat minimum they'll never fill, or how a small agency ends up exporting CSVs into QuickBooks every month because the tool they picked never actually did invoicing.

I've watched a freelance friend's invoicing process up close: tracked hours in one app, a monthly ritual of copying numbers into a spreadsheet, then formatting that into a PDF invoice by hand. It took about ninety minutes a month that a properly integrated tool would have collapsed into a five-minute review-and-send. That gap — between "has a time tracker" and "actually turns tracked time into a paid invoice without manual re-entry" — is the single biggest differentiator in this comparison, more than any single feature on a marketing page.

## Three things that actually matter when you're choosing

**1. Whether invoicing is a first-class feature or an afterthought.** Paymo, Harvest, and Hubstaff all generate client-ready invoices directly from tracked time, with recurring billing and expense tracking built in. Toggl Track added invoicing more recently, generated from its Summary Reports rather than being the tool's original core function. Everhour's invoicing lives behind its paid Team tier and leans on syncing tracked time into other tools (QuickBooks, Xero) rather than being a standalone invoice generator. If sending an invoice at the end of the month is a core part of the workflow — not just a nice-to-have — it's worth checking exactly how each tool gets from "tracked hour" to "PDF a client can pay," not just whether the word "invoicing" appears on the pricing page.

**2. Seat minimums, because they can quietly double the bill for a small team.** This is where the five diverge sharply. Everhour bills a minimum of 5 seats on its Team plan even if the actual team has two people — a two-person shop pays for five seats regardless. Harvest has no seat minimum on its paid tiers, and Hubstaff's current pricing page doesn't publish one either, so a solo user or two-person team should be able to pay only for the seats actually in use on either tool (worth a quick confirmation with Hubstaff's sales team before committing a specific headcount, since minimums have varied by plan in the past). Paymo splits the difference: its Solo plan is built for exactly one user, and the jump to Plus (unlimited seats, priced per user) only makes sense once there's an actual second person to add. Toggl Track's paid tiers are also priced per license with no stated minimum. For a freelancer or two-person team, this single detail can matter more than any per-seat price difference.

**3. Whether the tool is built around project structure or around monitoring.** Paymo, Harvest, and Toggl Track are organized around projects, tasks, and billable hours — the monitoring they do is limited to what's needed for accurate time tracking. Hubstaff leans harder into workforce monitoring: screenshots, activity-level percentages, and app/URL tracking are core features starting on its cheapest paid tier, which is a deliberate fit for teams managing distributed contractors who need accountability, and a poor fit for a freelancer who just wants to bill accurately for their own time without a screenshot tool watching them work.

## Which one fits your situation

**You're a solo freelancer who needs to track time and send invoices, nothing more:** Paymo's free tier or Solo plan. Unlimited invoicing on the free tier alone covers a single steady client relationship without paying anything.

**Your billing already runs through QuickBooks or Xero and you mainly need clean time-and-expense data feeding into it:** Harvest. The accounting integrations are more specific and mature here than in any other tool in this group.

**You already have a project management tool you like and just need accurate time tracking layered on top:** Toggl Track or Everhour, depending on which PM tool is already in use — Everhour if it's one of its natively supported tools (Asana, Trello, ClickUp, Basecamp), Toggl Track otherwise, given its broader 100+ integration list.

**You're managing a small team of contractors and need proof of hours worked, not just self-reported totals:** Hubstaff. The screenshot and activity-monitoring features solve a specific accountability problem the other four tools in this comparison don't attempt to address.

**You want project management, time tracking, and invoicing to be one system from day one, without stitching tools together:** Paymo, moving to the Plus tier once a second team member needs access — it's the only tool here where all three functions are original, first-class features rather than one function bolted onto another.

## Common questions

**Do any of these let me skip a separate invoicing tool entirely?**
Paymo, Harvest, and Hubstaff all generate invoices directly from tracked billable time without an export-and-reimport step. Toggl Track added invoicing based on its Summary Reports, which covers straightforward billing but is worth checking against a specific workflow (recurring billing, multi-currency clients) before assuming full parity with a dedicated invoicing tool. Everhour leans on syncing into external accounting tools rather than acting as the invoice generator itself.

**Is a 5-seat minimum, like Everhour's, actually common in this category?**
No — it's the exception in this comparison, not the rule. Paymo, Harvest, and Toggl Track all let a single user or small team pay only for the seats actually in use, and Hubstaff's published pricing doesn't list a minimum either, though it's worth confirming directly before committing a specific headcount. Either way, this matters most for a freelancer or a two-person shop not ready to commit to a fixed headcount.

**Which of these is the easiest to switch away from later if it doesn't work out?**
Tools with no seat minimum and a real free or trial tier (Paymo, Harvest) are the lowest-commitment starting points, since there's no minimum spend forcing a longer trial period than needed. Hubstaff's lack of any free plan makes it the highest-commitment option to start with, though its trial covers every feature so there's no artificial cap during the evaluation itself.

**Do I need the top-tier plan to get real project management features, not just time tracking?**
On Paymo specifically, yes for the deepest features — Gantt charts, task dependencies, and resource scheduling sit on the Pro tier, not Plus. Kanban boards and task calendars, however, are available from the entry Solo tier up, so a small team gets real project structure well before the top tier. On Harvest, no tier adds true project-management views (Gantt, kanban) — that's a structural limit of the tool, not a pricing-tier gate.

## Where to start

For anyone billing clients by the hour, the fastest way to feel the difference between these five is to track one real week of work in whichever free or trial option fits the team size, then try turning that week directly into a client invoice without leaving the tool. That single step — time tracked to invoice sent, with no CSV export in between — is where Paymo, Harvest, and Hubstaff separate themselves from Toggl Track and Everhour's more integration-dependent approach.

If the project management side matters as much as the billing side, start free with Paymo and build out a real project — not a test one — with actual tasks and a client attached. The free tier's 2-project cap is restrictive, but it's enough to see whether the combined project-and-invoicing workflow actually removes the manual re-entry step that makes separate tools painful in the first place. Only move to a paid plan once a specific limit — a third project, Gantt-level scheduling, or a second team member — is the thing actually standing in the way of getting work done.
